1

Arithmetic on a clock

It's 9 o'clock. What time will it be in 5 hours? In the States, nobody says 14. You go round the clock and land on 2. That's modular arithmetic: counting on a loop instead of a line. The number of positions on the loop is the modulus; on a 12-hour clock it's 12.

On a 24-hour clock, used in much of the world and by the military, 9:00 plus 5 hours really is 14:00. That clock is a loop too, just a longer one, so the wrap-around comes later: 14:00 plus 15 hours is 29, which lands on 5:00, because 29 mod 24 = 5. Same idea, different modulus.

Mathematicians start their clocks at 0, so a 12-hour clock has the positions 0 to 11 (the 24-hour clock already starts at 00:00). Try different clocks below. With a modulus of 7, the positions are the days of the week; with 2, every number is just “even” (0) or “odd” (1).

2

What “mod” means

a mod n is the remainder when you divide a by n. 14 ÷ 12 is 1 with 2 left over, so 14 mod 12 = 2. The “1” counts the full laps of the clock, and the remainder is where you end up.

Every whole number can be written as a = q × n + r, with r between 0 and n − 1. The remainder r is the answer. Negative numbers work too: going back 3 hours from 0 lands on 9, so −3 mod 12 = 9. (Some calculators and programming languages answer −3 instead; on a clock that's the same position.)

When two numbers land on the same position, we say they're congruent and write 14 ≡ 2 (mod 12), read “14 is congruent to 2, mod 12”. Every ciphertext, message and shared secret in the worksheets is a remainder like this.

Check yourself
  1. What is 100 mod 7?
    Show answer

    2, because 100 = 14 × 7 + 2.

  2. What is −1 mod 12?
    Show answer

    11. One step back from 0 on a 12-position clock lands on 11.

3

Clocks, calendars and odometers

You already do modular arithmetic all the time:

  • Hours are mod 12 or mod 24: 22:00 plus 5 hours is 27 mod 24 = 3:00.
  • Days of the week are mod 7. Number them Sunday = 0 to Saturday = 6, and “100 days after a Tuesday” is (2 + 100) mod 7 = 4, a Thursday.
  • Months are mod 12: 5 months after November (month 11) is 16 mod 12 = 4, April.
  • Odometers are mod 1,000,000: one more mile after 999,999 rolls over to 000,000.
  • Compass bearings are mod 360: turning 90° right from 300° gives 390 mod 360 = 30°.
  • The last digit of any number is that number mod 10, and even or odd is mod 2.
4

Multiplying, and when you can divide

Adding, subtracting and multiplying all work on a clock, and you can take the remainder whenever you like: before, during or after. (17 × 23) mod 10 is the same as (7 × 3) mod 10 = 1, which is why a computer can multiply 45-digit numbers mod n without them ever getting bigger than n2.

Division is different. Dividing by a means multiplying by a number x with a × x ≡ 1, called the inverse of a. In the table below, a row with a 1 in it has an inverse. Look at mod 10: the rows for 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8 never reach 1, because those numbers share a factor with 10. Now switch to mod 7: every row (except 0) has a 1, because 7 is prime. That's why so many cryptosystems work mod a prime.

Check yourself
  1. Mod 9, which numbers from 1 to 8 have an inverse?
    Show answer

    1, 2, 4, 5, 7 and 8: the ones that share no factor with 9. (3 and 6 share the factor 3.)

  2. What is the inverse of 2, mod 9?
    Show answer

    5, because 2 × 5 = 10 = 9 + 1. Switch the table to mod 9 and look along row 2.

5

Powers that go round in circles

Keep multiplying by the same number and the remainders eventually repeat. Powers of 3 mod 7 go 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1, and then start again. The length of the loop is the order of 3. Here, 3 visits every non-zero position before repeating, so it's called a generator.

Going forwards is easy: 35 mod 7 = 5. Going backwards, from “5” to “which power?”, is the discrete logarithm. Here you can just read it off the list. For a 45-digit prime the loop can be about 1045 steps long, far too long to list. In general that's what makes ElGamal, Diffie–Hellman and their cousins secure. Puzzles get around it by choosing primes with a special weakness; section 11 shows what it is.

Check yourself
  1. What is the order of 2, mod 11? Is 2 a generator?
    Show answer

    The powers go 2, 4, 8, 5, 10, 9, 7, 3, 6, 1, so the order is 10. That covers every number from 1 to 10, so yes, 2 is a generator mod 11.

  2. Using the same list, which power of 2 gives 9, mod 11?
    Show answer

    The 6th: 26 = 64 = 5 × 11 + 9. That backwards question is a discrete logarithm.

6

Fermat, Euler and φ

Fermat's little theorem. If p is prime and a isn't a multiple of p, then

a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p) example: 3^6 = 729 = 104 × 7 + 1

So every loop of powers mod a prime closes within p − 1 steps, and its length divides p − 1. Try it in section 5: every order mod 7 is 1, 2, 3 or 6.

Euler's φ (“phi”). φ(n) counts the numbers from 1 to n that share no factor with n. For n = 15 they are 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 11, 13 and 14, so φ(15) = 8. Two cases come up again and again:

φ(p) = p − 1 (p prime: every smaller number qualifies) φ(pq) = (p − 1)(q − 1) (p and q two different primes)

Why the second one: from 1 to pq, the numbers that do share a factor are the q multiples of p and the p multiples of q, and pq itself is counted in both lists. That leaves pq − q − p + 1 = (p − 1)(q − 1). For 15: 15 − 5 − 3 + 1 = 8.

Euler's theorem extends Fermat's to any modulus. If a shares no factor with n, then

a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) example: φ(15) = 8, and 2^8 = 256 = 17 × 15 + 1

Why RSA decryption works. RSA picks d so that e × d = 1 + k × φ(n) for some whole number k. Then

c^d = (m^e)^d = m^(1 + k·φ(n)) = m × (m^φ(n))^k ≡ m × 1^k = m (mod n)

Finding d needs φ(n), and finding φ(n) needs the primes p and q. That's why factoring breaks RSA.

Check yourself
  1. What is φ(21)? (21 = 3 × 7)
    Show answer

    (3 − 1)(7 − 1) = 12.

  2. Without multiplying it out, what is 512 mod 21?
    Show answer

    1, by Euler’s theorem: 5 shares no factor with 21, and 12 = φ(21).

7

Primes and factoring

A prime has no factors except 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, … Every whole number splits into primes in exactly one way, for example 360 = 23 × 32 × 5.

The greatest common divisor, gcd(a, b), is the biggest number that divides both. When it's 1, the numbers “share no factor” (they're coprime), and that's the condition behind inverses (section 4) and Euler's theorem (section 6). Euclid's algorithm finds it without factoring anything; section 8 shows the steps.

φ from the factors. Once you know the primes, φ is quick: multiply n by (1 − 1/p) for each different prime p. For 360: 360 × ½ × ⅔ × ⅘ = 96. With two primes this is the (p − 1)(q − 1) from section 6.

Why factoring is hard. The simplest method tries dividing by every number up to √n. That's instant for the 15-digit numbers below, but a 45-digit number would need about 1022 tries. Cleverer methods, like the quadratic sieve the worksheets use, split 45 digits in about a second. They still slow down steeply: the largest RSA challenge number factored so far, 250 digits, took about 2,700 CPU-core-years in 2020, and real keys are around 600 digits.

Two tools: fast powers and inverses

Square and multiply

Working out 7560 mod 561 by multiplying 7 by itself 559 times is slow, and a 45-digit exponent would take forever. Instead, square repeatedly (7, 72, 74, 78, …, taking the remainder each time), and multiply together the squares matching the 1s in the exponent written in binary. A 45-digit exponent needs only about 150 squarings. Big numbers work in these boxes.

Show every step

The extended Euclidean algorithm

To find an inverse, run Euclid's algorithm for the greatest common divisor: divide, keep the remainder, repeat. If the remainders reach 1, the inverse exists. Alongside each remainder the table keeps a multiplier, chosen so that

remainder ≡ multiplier × a (mod n)

Each new multiplier is the one from two rows up, minus “goes in” times the one from the row above. Start with 0 for n and 1 for a. On the row where the remainder is 1, the multiplier is the inverse. If it's negative, add n: for 7 mod 40 that row says −17, and −17 + 40 = 23. This is how RSA's private exponent d is found from e.

9

Two clocks at once

Picture a 3-hour clock and a 5-hour clock ticking together. Which count shows 2 on the first and 3 on the second? Counting up: 8 (8 mod 3 = 2, 8 mod 5 = 3). Because 3 and 5 share no factor, the pair of positions repeats only every 3 × 5 = 15 ticks, so 8 is the only answer between 0 and 14.

That's the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT): knowing a number modulo several moduli that share no factors pins it down modulo their product. Cryptosystems use it to split one big calculation into smaller ones, mod p and mod q, and glue the answers back together.

Check yourself
  1. Which x from 0 to 35 has x mod 4 = 3 and x mod 9 = 5?
    Show answer

    23: 23 = 5 × 4 + 3 and 23 = 2 × 9 + 5. Put 4, 3, 9, 5 in the boxes above to check.

Square roots on a clock

Which numbers x have x2 ≡ a (mod n)? Mod 7, squaring 1 to 6 gives 1, 4, 2, 2, 4, 1, so only 1, 2 and 4 have square roots. Those are called quadratic residues (squares, for short). Mod an odd prime, exactly half the non-zero numbers are squares, and each has two roots, x and p − x (on the clock, x and its mirror image).

Euler's criterion tells squares from non-squares in one power:

a^((p−1)/2) mod p = 1 if a is a square a^((p−1)/2) mod p = p − 1 if it isn't (that's −1) mod 7: 2^3 = 8 ≡ 1 so 2 is a square 3^3 = 27 ≡ 6 ≡ −1 so 3 isn't

The shortcut for primes that leave remainder 3 when divided by 4 (like 7, 11, 19, 23):

x = a^((p+1)/4) mod p √2 mod 7: 2^((7+1)/4) = 2^2 = 4, and 4^2 = 16 ≡ 2 ✓

It works because x2 = a(p+1)/2 = a × a(p−1)/2 ≡ a × 1 = a, using Euler's criterion. Other primes need a longer method (Tonelli–Shanks), which the worksheets do for you.

Mod a product of two primes, a square has four roots. Solve mod each prime, then glue the pieces together with the CRT (section 9). For x2 ≡ 1 (mod 15): mod 3 the roots are ±1, mod 5 they're ±1, and the four combinations are:

Check: 42 = 16, 112 = 121 and 142 = 196 all leave remainder 1 mod 15. Anyone can square, but finding all four roots needs the two primes. A system that encrypts by squaring rests on exactly that.

11

Why these discrete logs break

Finding x with gx ≡ h (mod p) is hard in general: the best general methods take about √p steps, around 1022 for a 45-digit prime. But the loop of powers of g has a length that divides p − 1 (section 6), and when p − 1 splits into small primes, the Pohlig–Hellman method solves the problem one small prime at a time:

  1. For each prime q dividing p − 1, raise both g and h to the power (p − 1)/q. That pushes both into a loop only q long, where only x mod q matters.
  2. Find x mod q by searching that short loop.
  3. Glue the answers together with the CRT (section 9).

Worked example. Find x with 3x ≡ 22 (mod 31). Here p − 1 = 30 = 2 × 3 × 5.

q330/q mod 312230/q mod 31The short loopx mod q
230301, 301
32551, 25, 52
51681, 16, 8, 4, 22

Read each answer from the loop's position: 2215 ≡ 30 is step 1 of the first loop, 5 is step 2 of the second, 8 is step 2 of the third. Then the CRT: x ≡ 1 (mod 2), x ≡ 2 (mod 3), x ≡ 2 (mod 5) gives x = 17, and indeed 317 mod 31 = 22.

What decides the difficulty is the largest prime factor of p − 1, not the size of p. A 45-digit prime whose p − 1 is built from primes of 13 digits or fewer needs only a few million steps per piece. Real systems avoid this by using “safe primes” p = 2q + 1 with q prime, so one piece is almost as big as p and the trick gains nothing. The worksheets show the pieces for any prime you give them.

Bonus

The day of the week for any date

The calendar is modular arithmetic too, and the mathematician John Conway turned that into a party trick you can do in your head: the Doomsday rule. Each year, a set of easy-to-remember dates all fall on the same day of the week, called that year's doomsday. Find it, then count from the nearest one.

The whole rule in one line
weekday = ( A(c) + S(y) + d − D(m) ) mod 7
  • A(c) the century's anchor day, from the first two digits of the year, c (Step 1)
  • S(y) the year's shift: how far the doomsday has moved since the century began, from the last two digits, y (Step 2)
  • d − D(m) how far your day d is from D(m), that month's doomsday date (Steps 3 and 4)

Every piece is just a count of days, and “mod 7” turns a count of days into a day of the week, exactly like the 7-position clock in section 1.

Number the days

Sunday = 0, Monday = 1, … Saturday = 6. Conway's memory aids: Noneday, Oneday, Twosday, Treblesday, Foursday, Fiveday, Six-a-day.

Step 1: the century's anchor, A(c)

A(c) = (5 × (c mod 4) + 2) mod 7 (c = the first two digits of the year)
c mod 45 × (c mod 4) + 2mod 7AnchorCenturies

2125Friday1800s, 2200s

Why it works. A century normally has 76 ordinary years and 24 leap years: 76 × 365 + 24 × 366 = 36,524 days, which is 5,217 weeks and 5 days. So each new century's doomsday lands 5 days later in the week, and on a 7-day clock, 5 forward is the same as 2 back. That's why the anchors step Tuesday → Sunday → Friday → Wednesday. The step into a year divisible by 400 (like 2000) also picks up that year's extra leap day, so it moves 6 instead of 5, and 5 + 5 + 5 + 6 = 21 days is exactly 3 weeks. The pattern therefore repeats every 400 years. In the formula, the “+ 2” is the starting point (2000 is a Tuesday, day 2), and the “5 ×” is that 5-day step per century.

To remember the two you'll use most: 1900 is a Wednesday (“We were born in the 1900s”), and 2000 is a Twosday (“Y2K”).

Step 2: the year's shift, S(y)

S(y) = y + ⌊y/4⌋
the definition
≡ ⌊y/12⌋ + (y mod 12) + ⌊(y mod 12)/4⌋
Conway's twelves
≡ (7 − (T₂ mod 7)) mod 7
“odd + 11” (T₂ is explained below)

y is the last two digits of the year, and ⌊ ⌋ means “round down”: ⌊26/4⌋ = 6. The three lines give different numbers (32, 4 and 4 for 2026), but the same remainder after dividing by 7, which is all that matters on a 7-day clock. That's what ≡ (mod 7) means. Use whichever you find easiest.

doomsday of the year = (A(c) + S(y)) mod 7

Why it works. An ordinary year is 365 days, which is 52 weeks and 1 day, so each year's doomsday falls 1 day later in the week than the last. A leap year has 366 days, so the doomsday jumps 2. From the start of the century to year y, that's y one-day steps, plus one extra for each leap year along the way, and there are ⌊y/4⌋ of those. (A leap year's own extra day counts here, because its February 29 comes before that year's doomsday dates from March onward; Step 3 deals with January and February.)

For 2026: S(26) = 26 + ⌊26/4⌋ = 26 + 6 = 32, and 32 mod 7 = 4. Tuesday (2) + 4 = day 6, Saturday, the doomsday for 2026.

Where the shortcuts come from. Both are y + ⌊y/4⌋ rearranged to keep the numbers small:

  • Conway's twelves. Write y = 12a + r (a twelves, remainder r). Then y + ⌊y/4⌋ = 12a + r + 3a + ⌊r/4⌋ = 15a + r + ⌊r/4⌋. Since 15 = 14 + 1 and 14 is two whole weeks, 15a counts the same as a:
    S(y) ≡ a + r + ⌊r/4⌋ 2026: a = 2, r = 2, ⌊2/4⌋ = 0 → 2 + 2 + 0 = 4
  • “Odd + 11”. The same total, rearranged so you only ever halve and add 11:
    T₁ = y, plus 11 if y is odd T₂ = T₁ / 2, plus 11 if that's odd S(y) ≡ (7 − (T₂ mod 7)) mod 7 2026: T₁ = 26 (even, so no +11) T₂ = 26 / 2 = 13, odd, so 13 + 11 = 24 S ≡ 7 − (24 mod 7) = 7 − 3 = 4

January and February in a leap year

The year's doomsday, A(c) + S(y), already includes that year's February 29. That's right for March to December, which come after the leap day. January and February come before it, so in a leap year their doomsday dates move one day later:

January doomsdaysFebruary doomsdays
Common year3, 10, 17, 24, 317, 14, 21, 28
Leap year4, 11, 18, 251, 8, 15, 22, 29

Why. The last day of February is always a doomsday, because March 14 is exactly 2 weeks later (it's “March 0”). In a leap year the last day is the 29th, not the 28th, so every January and February doomsday date shifts with it. Count back 8 weeks (56 days) from the last day of February and you land on January 3 in a common year, or January 4 in a leap year.

Example: January 1, 2024. 2024 is a leap year. A = 2, S(24) = 24 + 6 = 30, so the doomsday is (2 + 30) mod 7 = 4, Thursday. With the leap-year date D(1) = 4: (4 + 1 − 4) mod 7 = 1, Monday, which is correct. Using 3 by mistake gives Tuesday, one day off. That's the most common slip with this rule.

Is it a leap year? If the year is divisible by 4, yes, except century years, which are leap years only when divisible by 400. So 2024 and 2000 were leap years; 1900 and 2100 aren't.

Step 4: count to your date, d − D(m)

weekday = (doomsday of the year + d − D(m)) mod 7

Christmas 2026: the year's doomsday is Saturday (6), and December's doomsday date is the 12th, so (6 + 25 − 12) mod 7 = 19 mod 7 = 5, a Friday. Going backwards works the same way: December 1 gives (6 + 1 − 12) mod 7 = −5 mod 7 = 2, a Tuesday. (−5 mod 7 is 2, just as going back 5 hours on a 7-position clock lands on 2.)

All in one line, for Christmas 2026: 2 + 32 + 25 − 12 = 47, and 47 mod 7 = 5, Friday.

The rule uses the Gregorian calendar, which Spain, Portugal, Italy, Poland and France adopted in 1582, other countries over the following centuries, and Britain and its colonies in 1752. Earlier dates in those places were recorded in the Julian calendar, so a historical date may not match.

    The same answer from one formula (Zeller's congruence)

    Christian Zeller's 1882 formula does the whole thing in one line. Treat January and February as months 13 and 14 of the previous year. With q the day, m the month, K the year's last two digits and J its first two:

    h = (q + ⌊13(m + 1)/5⌋ + K + ⌊K/4⌋ + ⌊J/4⌋ + 5J) mod 7 (0 = Saturday, 1 = Sunday, …)
