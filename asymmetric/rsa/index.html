How RSA works two minutes, with tiny numbers

RSA uses two keys. The public key locks a message and anyone can have it. The private key unlocks it, and only the receiver should have it. The two are linked by a calculation that's easy one way and very hard the other.

Making the keys

  1. Pick two secret prime numbers, p and q, and multiply them: n = p × q. The number n is public.
  2. Work out φ(n) = (p − 1) × (q − 1), read "phi of n". It's the count of numbers below n that share no factor with it, and it's what lets the two exponents cancel out.
  3. Pick a public exponent e that shares no factor with φ(n). Almost everyone uses e = 65537. The pair (n, e) is the public key.
  4. Find the private exponent d: the number where e × d leaves remainder 1 when divided by φ(n). That's written d = e−1 mod φ(n) and called the inverse of e.

Locking and unlocking

  1. The message is turned into a number m smaller than n.
  2. Encrypt: c = me mod n. ("mod n" means "the remainder after dividing by n".)
  3. Decrypt: m = cd mod n. This works because e × d is one more than a multiple of φ(n), and Euler's theorem says raising to a multiple of φ(n) brings you back to 1.

Why it's safe, and why a puzzle isn't

Everyone can see n and e. Getting d needs φ(n), which needs p and q, which means splitting n back into its two primes. For the 600-digit n used in real life, no computer on Earth can do that. For the 40 or 50 digits a puzzle can use, this page does it in about a second, and that's the step that breaks the puzzle open.

The whole thing with tiny numbers

              Step 2

              Split n into p × q

              This is the step RSA is built to make impossible: finding the two primes that multiply to n. At puzzle size, a computer can do it.

                How does this page factor n?

                First it tries dividing by every small prime, which catches typos fast, since a mistyped n usually has small factors. Then it tries Fermat's method, which is instant when p and q are close together. If neither works, it runs a quadratic sieve. The sieve collects hundreds of numbers made only of small primes, then combines them into a pair of squares that gives up a factor.

                The sieve's running time grows fast with the size of n: about a second at 45 digits, tens of seconds at 55, years at 120, and far longer than the age of the universe at real-world sizes.

                Step 3

                φ(n): the number that links the keys

                With p and q known, Euler's totient is quick: φ(n) = (p − 1) × (q − 1).

                φ or λ? Why two people can get different d values

                Some write-ups use the Carmichael function λ(n) = lcm(p − 1, q − 1) in place of φ(n). It's a smaller number that does the same job, so the d it gives is smaller too. Both d values decrypt every message to exactly the same answer, so neither one is wrong.

                Step 4

                The private exponent d

                d is the inverse of e modulo φ(n): the number that makes e × d leave remainder 1 when divided by φ(n). The extended Euclidean algorithm finds it.

                  Show the extended Euclidean algorithm

                  Divide, keep the remainder, and repeat until the remainder is 0, the same way you'd find a greatest common divisor. If the last non-zero remainder is 1, e and φ(n) share no factor and d exists. Working back up these rows gives d.

                  Step 5

                  Decrypt: m = cd mod n

                  Raise the ciphertext to the private exponent and keep the remainder after dividing by n. With numbers this size, the page does it by repeated squaring, which takes a few hundred multiplications instead of an impossible number.

                    Step 6

                    Read the message

                      If none of these look like anything

                      Decryption scrambles everything when even one digit of c, n, or e is off, so a wrong input doesn't give a slightly wrong answer; it gives noise. Recount the digits of each number against the puzzle (the counts are shown next to every box).

                      If the inputs are right, the message may use a different encoding: the puzzle text usually says which one. Lining the blocks up from the end helps when a short message is tacked onto the end of a longer one.
