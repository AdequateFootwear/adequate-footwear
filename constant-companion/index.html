Constant Companion

Hundreds of mathematical, physical, astronomical and chemical constants, with their values, the symbols each one is written with, a search by name, by symbol or by the digits you have, and a small calculator for sums with them.

An optional helper. It runs entirely in your browser and keeps working once it has loaded, even with no signal. What you type stays on this device.

4

Where the values come from

Mathematical constants were computed from their definitions at 120 significant digits and are shown to 100 digits, cut off rather than rounded, so the digits you see are exactly the start of the true expansion. Each was also computed a second, independent way, with a different formula wherever one exists, and the two agree to at least 110 digits. A continued fraction shows only the terms those digits settle for certain.

Physical constants are the complete CODATA 2022 set of recommended values, with their standard uncertainties. A value written 6.674 30(15) means 6.674 30 with a standard uncertainty of 0.000 15 in its last digits. Since 2019 the constants that define the SI (among them c, h, e, k and N_A) are exact. When CODATA prints an exact value cut short, as in 1.054 571 817…, the further digits shown here were worked out from those exact defining constants, and they agree with every digit CODATA prints.

Astronomical and Earth constants come from the International Astronomical Union’s resolutions (the 2012 astronomical unit, the 2015 nominal solar and planetary values, the 2015 parsec), the IERS and IAU time scales, and the WGS 84 ellipsoid that GPS uses. A nominal value is an exact conversion constant, not a measurement. Each card names its source.

Chemistry and everyday constants are the usual reference states (absolute zero, the triple and critical points of water, standard temperature and pressure in both conventions) and units that are defined exactly, such as the inch, the pound and the calorie.

The calculator reads a sum the way Higher Powers does and works it out with the same engine, carrying more digits than it shows. A mathematical constant goes in with all of its 100 digits, an exact constant exactly, and a measured one with the digits CODATA gives. A result shows only the digits its inputs vouch for. When a sum uses measured constants, the note under the result lists their relative standard uncertainties and a rough combined figure: each input is nudged by its own uncertainty and the effects are added as if the inputs were independent. CODATA’s values are partly correlated, so treat that figure as a guide, not a full uncertainty analysis.

Send to Higher Powers opens Higher Powers in this tab with the constant set as a variable, under the name you choose. The name and the value travel only in the page’s address, after the #: nothing is sent anywhere, and nothing leaves your device. A value whose digits go on is sent ending in …, so Higher Powers, like this calculator, shows only the digits it can vouch for.

How digit searches match. Only the digits count: “1.6180”, “16180” and “0.0016180” all look for the run 1 6 1 8 0. A value that starts with your digits comes first, then one that rounds to them in its last place, then one that has them somewhere further along (for four digits or more). If you typed a decimal point or a power of ten, values of the same size rank above the rest.
