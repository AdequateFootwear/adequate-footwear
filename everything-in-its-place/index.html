Everything in Its Place

For when things need to be just so.

An optional helper for arranging a room or a home the feng shui way: map the bagua onto a floor plan, look up what each area stands for, see the five elements, and work out a Kua number. It runs entirely in your browser and keeps working once it has loaded, even with no signal. Anything you draw or type stays on this device.

The floor plan

Choose the kind of space and a sample plan appears, turned so the front door is on the wall you pick, with the bagua’s nine areas laid over it. Tap any spot on the plan to see which area it falls in, or click the plan and use the arrow keys. You can also use a plain rectangle, or put a picture of your own plan underneath.

Commonly suggested here

    The room, the door and north

    0° means north is toward the top of the drawing, 90° toward its right-hand side. Drag the needle, click it and use the arrow keys (Shift for 15° steps), or type a number. To find it, stand in the space holding a compass or phone, and line it up with the walls. Phone compasses show magnetic north, which can be a few degrees from true north.

    Fit the grid

    Drag the grid on the plan to move it, or drag a corner dot to resize it. A tap without dragging still shows the area. Or type the grid’s position here, in the same units as the room.

    A picture of your own floor plan (optional)

    Put a picture of a plan under the drawing, then fit the grid to its outline. Choosing “A plain rectangle” above keeps a sample plan out of the way. The picture is read on this device and never uploaded. It isn’t saved, so add it again next time.

    The five elements

    Each area belongs to one of five elements. In the generating cycle each element feeds the next; in the controlling cycle each one keeps another in check. Choose an element to see how it relates to the rest.

    Generating (feeding)

    1. Wood feeds Fire: it burns.
    2. Fire makes Earth: it leaves ash.
    3. Earth bears Metal: ores form in it.
    4. Metal carries Water: it gathers as condensation (and minerals enrich springs).
    5. Water nourishes Wood: it makes plants grow.

    Controlling (checking)

    1. Wood parts Earth: roots break up the soil.
    2. Earth contains Water: banks and dams hold it.
    3. Water quenches Fire.
    4. Fire melts Metal.
    5. Metal cuts Wood.
    ElementColoursShapesMaterialsDirectionsSeason

    To strengthen an area, people commonly add its own element and the one that feeds it, and go easy on the element that controls it. Sources differ a little on materials: stone, for example, is Earth in some books and Metal in others.

    4

    Kua number

    The Kua number comes from the year you were born, counted the traditional way: the solar year begins at Li Chun, “the start of spring”, around 4 February. Traditional texts give the formula in two versions, one traditionally used for people assigned male at birth (shown here as Version A) and one for people assigned female at birth (Version B). Both are shown side by side, and the page never asks which applies.

    5

    About

    Two schools. The Black Hat school (also called BTB, or Western feng shui), developed by Thomas Lin Yun and popularised in the United States from the 1970s and 1980s, lines the bagua up with the front door: whatever the compass says, the wall with the door always holds Knowledge, Career and Helpful People, from left to right as you walk in. It can be laid over a whole home or over a single room, using that room’s door. The classical compass schools, such as Eight Mansions and Flying Stars, use the real compass directions instead, so each area sits where its direction is. The two methods only agree when the front door is on the north side of the home. Neither is “right”; pick one and use it consistently.

    The areas. Each of the eight outer areas belongs to a trigram of the I Ching in the Later Heaven arrangement (attributed to King Wen), which fixes its compass direction, element, family member, season and body part. The family members, body parts and seasons follow the traditional commentary on the trigrams; the centre has no trigram, so it has none of these. The names of the areas, and the colours and things suggested for them, vary from book to book. This page uses one common set and keeps it the same everywhere.

    Lo Shu. The Lo Shu is the 3 × 3 magic square whose rows, columns and diagonals each add up to 15. Each area takes the number in its place. Chinese diagrams put south at the top, so it reads:

    4SE9S2SW
    3E5Centre7W
    8NE1N6NW

    The Kua formula. Take the solar year (the year before, if the birthday falls before Li Chun) and add up its digits until one digit is left. For Version A, take that digit from 11, and if you get 10, add those digits too (10 becomes 1). For Version B, add 4 to it, and if the total is more than 9, add its digits too. If either comes to 5, which has no trigram of its own, Version A uses 2 and Version B uses 8. This matches the familiar shortcut that uses only the last two digits (for 1900 to 1999, take that digit from 10, or add 5; from 2000, take it from 9, or add 6), and works for any year. The page’s results were checked against the standard tables for every year from 1900 to 2099.

    Groups and directions. Kua numbers 1, 3, 4 and 9 make up the East group, whose favourable directions are north, south, east and southeast; 2, 6, 7 and 8 make up the West group, with west, northwest, southwest and northeast. Each number’s eight directions come from the Eight Mansions method: changing the lines of its trigram one at a time, top, middle, bottom, middle and so on, steps through them in a fixed order.

    Li Chun. Li Chun is the moment the Sun reaches 315° along the ecliptic, measured from the spring equinox, halfway between the winter solstice and the spring equinox. The exact day varies from year to year, falling on 3, 4 or 5 February. The page works it out from the Sun’s position, to within a minute or two, and gives the date in China Standard Time (UTC+8), as the Chinese almanac does. Some practitioners simply use 4 February every year, and some use the moment in their own time zone, so a birthday right at the turn can be read either way.

    This page describes traditional associations as they are commonly taught. It doesn’t claim anything about their effects, and it isn’t a substitute for a consultation.
