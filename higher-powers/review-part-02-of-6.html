Higher Powers

A calculator for exact whole numbers and fractions, high-precision decimals and complex numbers, digit sums and digital roots, ranges, letter values, coordinate offsets, and a table that runs one formula over many rows. Each result shows how the line was read.

An optional helper. It works entirely in your browser and keeps working once it has loaded, even with no signal. Nothing you type leaves this page; your work is kept only in this browser.

1

The calculator

Type a calculation; the result appears as you type, along with exactly how it was read. Whole numbers and fractions stay exact, whatever their size. Add as many lines as you like.

The usual ways of writing things work too, such as sin⁻¹ x, sin² x, |x|, log₂ 8 and 5C2, and a short note says when one was applied. To find how to type a symbol, see Symbols and functions.

Angles
Show

Digits: how many significant digits a decimal result shows, from 20 to 200. Exact results are always shown in full.

Digit sums of negative numbers and decimals

digitsum and digitalroot ask what you want the first time they meet a negative number or a decimal. Your choices are kept here, and you can change them.

2

Variables

Give names values: letters (A, b), Greek letters (φ, Ω) or words (steps). A value can be a number or a calculation that uses other variables. Change one and everything that uses it updates. Names are case-sensitive, so A and a are different.

3

Table: one formula, many rows

Write a formula, then fill in a row for each set of values. The columns are the formula’s variables, and the result column updates as you type.

Paste rows

One row per line, with the values separated by tabs (as when copying from a spreadsheet) or commas, in the column order shown in the table. You can also paste several values straight into a cell.

4

Ranges and lists

The middle of a range, and the mean and median of a list. Each box takes a number or a calculation.

Separate the items with commas, semicolons or new lines (or just spaces, if no item contains one).

5

Letter values

Each letter’s place in the alphabet (A = 1 to Z = 26), and their sum for each word and for the whole phrase. Accented letters count as their plain letter (É as E); anything else is skipped.

6

Coordinate offset

Add a number to, or take it from, the minutes or the degrees of one half of a coordinate. The result is rounded to a thousandth of a minute, and minutes always stay under 60, carrying into the degrees.

In degrees and decimal minutes (N 45° 12.345 W 093° 54.321), or in decimal degrees (45.20575, -93.90535).

7

Symbols and functions

How to type what you see in print. Search by how it looks (√, sin⁻¹, |x|, ⌊x⌋) or by its name (square root, floor). Tap what to type to put it into the box you were last typing in, or copy it.

8

How it works

Exact numbers. Whole numbers and fractions are kept exactly, however large: 21000 has all 302 of its digits, and 1/3 + 1/6 is exactly 1/2. A decimal you type, such as 0.1, is the exact fraction 1/10, so 0.1 + 0.2 is exactly 0.3. Results that can’t be fractions, such as √2 or π, are decimals.

Decimals. Square roots, powers, logarithms, trigonometry and the rest are worked out with high-precision arithmetic written for this page, at the number of digits you choose plus a dozen or so more, and every decimal carries a bound on its own error. That bound decides how many digits are shown: if a calculation loses precision (subtracting two nearly equal numbers, say), the result shows only the digits that are still right and says so. A difference that cancels completely, such as eiπ + 1, is shown as 0, “to within the working precision”. Values tested against an independent high-precision library agreed to every digit shown.

Complex numbers. Square roots, logarithms, powers and the inverse trigonometric and hyperbolic functions of negative and complex numbers give their principal values, with the usual branch cuts. When the imaginary part of a result is too small to show at the chosen number of digits, the result is shown as a real number.

How lines are read. Besides plain function calls, the calculator understands the usual printed forms: sin⁻¹ x is the inverse sine, not 1/sin x; sin² x is (sin x)²; a function without brackets takes the next number, name or bracket, so sin 2x is sin(2x); log₂ 8 is log(8, 2); |x|, ⌊x⌋ and ⌈x⌉ are abs, floor and ceil; 5C2 and C(5, 2) are combinations; and n!! is the double factorial. “Read as” always shows the result of this, and a note says which convention was used. Where a form could mean more than one thing, such as lg, or bars inside bars, it asks for brackets or another spelling rather than guessing.

Primes. Below 3.3 × 1024, isprime is certain. Above that it uses the Baillie–PSW test, which no known composite number passes, and it says “probable prime”. factor finds small factors quickly; if part of a large number can’t be split within a few seconds, it says so.

Limits. Whole numbers stay exact up to 100,000 digits, and decimals reach from about 10−100000 to 10100000.
