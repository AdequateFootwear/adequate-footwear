Where are ewe going? Tap me for a palindrome.

The ewe is a drawing of a fluffy sheep in a field beside a signpost. Tap her, or press her with the keyboard, and she says a palindrome: a sentence whose letters read the same backwards.

Step 1

The pattern

Type how the three six-digit codes are built: a letter for each digit that comes from a variable, and a digit for each one that’s fixed. A run of the same letter is one variable, and its length is how many digits that variable has. Letters can be capitals or not; they’re shown as capitals.

    The variables

    Each value is a whole number. You can also type a small sum, such as 12 × 37 + 5 or (800 − 6) ÷ 2, using + − × ÷ (or * and /) and brackets.

    You’re typing the codes yourself in step 2, so the pattern is skipped. Nothing you typed here is lost.

      Step 3

      Where they point

      The coordinates appear here once all 18 digits are known.

        How the codes become coordinates step by step

        Number the 18 digits across the three codes: code 1 holds digits 1 to 6, code 2 digits 7 to 12, and code 3 digits 13 to 18. Three of them aren’t part of the coordinates at all: digit 4 gives the quadrant, and digits 9 and 15 are check digits.

        Once all 18 digits are known, the working for your codes appears here.

        The method

        1. Quadrant. Digit 4 says which hemispheres: 1 is north and east, 2 is south and east, 3 is north and west, 4 is south and west. Anything else isn’t a quadrant.
        2. Parity test. Add digit 14 and digit 17 (code 3, positions 2 and 5). The sum being even or odd picks the digit order.
        3. Digit order. When the sum is even, the latitude is digits 3 11 . 8 16 1 17 6 and the longitude is digits 2 13 18 . 10 7 5 14 12. When it’s odd, the latitude is digits 7 6 . 3 13 16 17 1 and the longitude is digits 11 18 5 . 2 10 12 14 8. Both are decimal degrees, to five places.
        4. Check digits. Digit 9 checks digits 1 to 8, and digit 15 checks digits 10 to 14 and 16 to 18. Multiply the eight digits, in that order, by 8, 6, 4, 2, 3, 5, 9 and 7, add them up, and take the remainder after dividing by 11. Eleven minus that remainder is the check digit, except that 10 counts as 0 and 11 counts as 5.

        The check digits only catch mistakes; they don’t change where the codes point. They catch almost every single wrong digit, but because two different remainders both lead to 5, now and then a typo slips past, so a passing check isn’t a guarantee.

        How the pattern fills the codes

        Each variable’s digits go into its letters from left to right. A variable whose letter appears in more than one place, such as P in PPPQQQ RRRRRR SRRUUP, has as many digits as it has letters in all, and they fill its places in order of appearance: the first digits go into its first place, the next into its second place, and so on. Digits typed in the pattern stay as they are.

            How coordinates become codes step by step

            Encoding runs the decoding method backwards. The digits are numbered 1 to 18 across the three codes: code 1 holds digits 1 to 6, code 2 digits 7 to 12, and code 3 digits 13 to 18.

            Once the coordinates read cleanly, the working for them appears here.

            The method

            1. Round. The codes carry each coordinate in decimal degrees to exactly five places, so the latitude is written as 7 digits (dd.ddddd) and the longitude as 8 (ddd.ddddd). Anything finer is rounded to the nearest 0.00001°, with an exact half rounding up. On the ground that’s a step of about a metre or less.
            2. Quadrant. Digit 4 is 1 for north and east, 2 for south and east, 3 for north and west, and 4 for south and west. A latitude or longitude that rounds to 0 has no hemisphere, and counts as north or east.
            3. Digit order. Both digit orders put the latitude’s 6th digit in digit 17 and the longitude’s 7th digit in digit 14, and the decoder adds digits 14 and 17 to choose its order. So if those two coordinate digits add up to an even number, the even order is used: the latitude goes into digits 3 11 . 8 16 1 17 6 and the longitude into digits 2 13 18 . 10 7 5 14 12. If they add up to an odd number, the latitude goes into digits 7 6 . 3 13 16 17 1 and the longitude into digits 11 18 5 . 2 10 12 14 8. Exactly one order fits every coordinate.
            4. Check digits. Digit 9 is worked out from digits 1 to 8, and digit 15 from digits 10 to 14 and 16 to 18: multiply the eight digits, in that order, by 8, 6, 4, 2, 3, 5, 9 and 7, add them up, and take the remainder after dividing by 11. Eleven minus that remainder is the check digit, except that 10 counts as 0 and 11 counts as 5.
