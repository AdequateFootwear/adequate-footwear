The garden

A walk up the critical line

Press the daisy for a fact.

The Riemann zeta function is ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + …, extended to every complex s except the pole at s = 1. Its trivial zeros are at −2, −4, −6, …; all its other zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < Re(s) < 1, and every one ever found lies on the critical line Re(s) = ½, at s = ½ + it. In this garden a flower marks each of those zeros.

Along the line

Follow ζ(½ + it) up the critical line. Z(t) is the real-valued function with |Z(t)| = |ζ(½ + it)|, so its zeros are exactly the zeros on the line. A flower blooms at each, with its number.

Point at the plot, or tap it, to read the values. With the keyboard, focus the plot and use ← and → to move (hold Shift for bigger steps), Page Up and Page Down to jump between zeros, and Home and End for the ends.

As t climbs, the point ζ(½ + it) traces this curve. It passes through the origin, where the flower is, at every zero. The open circle is the start of the stretch and the open square its end; the filled dot follows the point you are reading.

Domain colouring

A picture of ζ(s) over a rectangle of the complex plane. The hue is the argument of ζ(s): red where it is a positive real number (0°), then green at 120° and blue at 240°. The brightness comes from the size: dark near 0, full colour where |ζ(s)| = 1, and pale toward white where it is large. Each zero is a dark point where all the colours meet, and the pole at s = 1 is a white one.

5

About the numbers

ζ(s) anywhere. For Re(s) ≥ 0, and just left of 0, the page uses Euler–Maclaurin summation: the first N terms of the series, then a correction built from Bernoulli numbers, with N and the number of corrections chosen from |s| so the leftover is below double precision. For Re(s) < 0 it uses the functional equation ζ(s) = 2sπs−1 sin(πs/2) Γ(1 − s) ζ(1 − s), worked in logarithms so very large values don’t overflow (high up the plane, a little left of 0, the summation is used directly because it is more accurate there). Γ comes from Stirling’s series. Checked against an independent high-precision library at a few hundred points, values agree to about 14 significant digits on the right half of the plane, and to 10 or more far to the left and high up. Close to a zero, the error is a tiny absolute amount (about 10−15), not a fraction of the value; next to the pole at s = 1 it is a tiny fraction of |ζ(s)|, so a small real part there is shown with fewer digits. Next to the pole, Re(s) − 1 is worked out from the decimal you type, before any rounding.

On the critical line. ζ(½ + it) = Z(t) e−iθ(t), where θ is the Riemann–Siegel theta function. Above t = 200 the page computes Z(t) with the Riemann–Siegel formula and its first five correction terms, C0 to C4; Gabcke’s bound for what is left is 0.017 (t/2π)−11/4. Where that isn’t small enough for a zero’s last digits, it uses Euler–Maclaurin instead. Phases such as t ln n and θ(t) are carried in double-double arithmetic (about 32 digits), because at large t ordinary double precision would lose the digits that matter.

Finding zero number n. The Gram points gk solve θ(gk) = kπ. Gram’s law says zero number n usually lies between gn−2 and gn−1; it first fails at g126. So the page groups the Gram intervals into Gram blocks (between “good” Gram points, where (−1)kZ(gk) > 0) and looks inside each block, sampling more and more finely, until it has found as many sign changes of Z as the block has intervals (Rosser’s rule). That finds close pairs such as Lehmer’s near t ≈ 7005. The count is then confirmed: above t = 800 with Turing’s method in Brent’s form (enough Gram blocks on each side that obey Rosser’s rule pin down how many zeros lie below a Gram point exactly), and below that with Backlund’s test (Re ζ(σ + ig) > 0 for every σ ≥ ½, checked at closely spaced points). If a block won’t give up its zeros, the search widens to its neighbours. Each zero is then pinned down by a bracketing root finder that can’t leave its sign change, and polished in double-double.

How accurate. Checked against an independent high-precision library for zeros 1 to 2,000 and a few hundred more up to 10,000,000, including Gram’s first failure and Lehmer’s pair. The largest difference found was about 10−14, far below the decimals shown. Each zero is shown with as many decimals as its own error estimate supports, never fewer than 10.

The pictures. The plots sample Z(t) closely enough to follow every wiggle; the values you read off when you point are recomputed at full accuracy. The domain colouring uses a quicker, lower-accuracy evaluation per pixel (good to about 9 digits, far more than a colour can show) and is drawn a little at a time, coarse first, so the page stays responsive.
